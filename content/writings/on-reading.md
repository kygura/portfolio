Reading gets treated as a virtue in itself, and the number of books read as a measure of a mind.

I like reading. It can be useful, productive and a real pleasure, and it's worth doing for its own sake the way music or long walks are. What I object to is the idea that reading, on its own, makes you think.

Most of the time it does the opposite. When you read, someone else has already done the work. They picked the question, set the terms, put the steps in order and reached the conclusion, and all you do is follow along. Nothing in the act forces you to stop and ask whether any of it is true. You can get through three hundred pages nodding the whole way and never once set your own mind against the text. Unless you're actively fighting what's on the page, you're taking in another person's ideas in the order they chose, at the pace they set.

The better the book, the worse this gets. A bad book bounces off you: it's clumsy, you notice the holes and move on. A brilliant book persuades you and leaves a mark. You finish it impressed, and impressed people adopt what impressed them. Weeks later you catch yourself repeating its conclusions as if they were your own, except you never did the thinking that would have earned them. You skipped straight to the answer and now you're carrying borrowed material around as a worldview.

This is how people end up with plenty of opinions and no original thoughts. Bring up any subject and they'll hand you a fully formed position, sourced from whichever author or podcaster got to them first. Press on it and there's nothing underneath, because they never built it. They're NPCs running someone else's dialogue tree. Ask why they believe something and the honest answer is that they read it somewhere and it sounded right.

The size of their bookshelf tells you nothing about any of this, though BookTok and the rest of the online reading crowd have turned it into a sport anyway. There are Goodreads challenges, monthly wrap-ups, shelves sorted by spine color, and annotation tabs in five pastel shades. The books themselves are almost beside the point. What counts is the stack, the count and the photo of the stack. Half of it is the same romantasy series pushed by the same forty accounts, and the reviews are pure vibes: it destroyed me, I'm unwell, five stars. You get a whole community built around reading where you'd struggle to find one person who disagreed with a book. Plenty of heavy readers have never had an idea that didn't come out of a book, while some of the sharpest people around have read maybe ten and spent years turning each one over.

Good reading looks like an argument. You read a paragraph and stop. You ask whether it holds up, what it assumes, what it leaves out, and what you'd have said if nobody had told you anything. You put the book down for a day because one claim keeps bothering you. You disagree in the margins. When you finish, you can say which parts you accept and why, and which you throw out. Read that way, a book sharpens your own thinking.

Thinking doesn't need a book at all. You can sit with a question on a walk or at three in the morning and get further than a week of reading would take you, because the whole time the work is yours. Reading can serve that work, and it can just as easily stand in for it.

I'd be a hypocrite to lean on anyone for any of this, but others reached the same place long before me, and the overlap interests me.

The oldest version I know of comes from Zhuangzi. A duke is reading in his hall while a wheelwright named Pian works below. Pian asks what he's reading. The words of the sages, says the duke. Are the sages still alive? No, they're dead. Then what you're reading is the dregs of dead men. The duke threatens to have him executed unless he can explain himself, so Pian talks about his trade. He can feel when a wheel is cut right, neither too loose nor too tight, but he can't put that feeling into words, not even for his own son, which is why he's still making wheels at seventy. Whatever the sages actually knew died with them, and the book is what was left over.

Socrates distrusted writing because he thought it would give people the look of wisdom without the substance. Seneca warned that reading too many authors scatters the mind. Montaigne said you can be learned with someone else's learning but wise only with your own. Descartes gave up on books and went to read what he called the great book of the world. Kant thought that having a book understand things for you was a kind of self-imposed immaturity. Emerson complained about meek young men who grow up in libraries believing their job is to accept what Cicero and Locke concluded, forgetting that Cicero and Locke were once young men in libraries themselves. Schopenhauer called reading thinking with someone else's head, and Nietzsche considered reading a book first thing in the morning a vice.
