> _"Absorb what is useful, discard what is useless, add what is specifically your own."_
> Bruce Lee

Ninety percent of everything is crap. Theodore Sturgeon said it about science fiction, and it holds just as well for music, film, games, books, jobs, conversations and people. I find it a relief.

If most things were good, every album, every series and every person asking for my time would have a fair claim on it, and a lifetime wouldn't be enough to answer them all. Knowing that nine out of ten things are filler cancels the debt. I can put down the book that bores me by page fifty, leave unwatched the series everyone is talking about, and see less of the people who leave me emptier than they found me.

This only works with taste, and taste has to be earned. Nobody can adopt someone else's ten percent. You have to go through enough of the ninety yourself to know its texture and to recognize the rare thing when it appears. With that behind you, discarding becomes a matter of conviction instead of snobbery, because you know exactly what you're throwing away.

The same goes for people. Most of the ones you meet are dull and leave you as they found you, or a little worse. A few bring out the best in you and make you want to be more. Those deserve your time, and the rest can remain pleasant acquaintances at a distance.

The modern instinct is to accumulate: more books logged, more films watched, more friends, more experiences. Accumulating like that is gluttony. To live well is to choose.

Bruce Lee's line holds the whole method. Absorbing and discarding are curation. Adding what is specifically your own is the hard part, and it is the part that makes someone a person.

You can absorb and discard forever and still end up a collage of other people, with better sources to copy and nothing more. The third step means taking what survived the filter and making from it something that didn't exist before. Every view you hold should be one you can stand behind without pointing to where you picked it up. When you speak, it should be you speaking: your doubts, your commitments, the impulses that pull against each other. A real person is full of paradoxes. A borrowed self comes out neat and consistent, because someone else did the editing.

The same ratio holds for your own life. Most of what you do leads nowhere. Most days are spent going through the motions, with work that doesn't compound, conversations you forget and plans that never come to anything. Maybe eighty percent of it, maybe ninety. A small slice of what you do and live through will change you, and those are the moments you'll remember: the book, the person, the trip, the decision that turned everything. You can't skip straight to them, and you can't predict which ones they'll be. You can only stay sharp enough to recognize them and keep enough room in your life to take them in.

Those moments don't come out of nowhere. Most of life runs in compression, the ninety percent: long stretches where you grind, nothing seems to move, and the work feels pointless while you're doing it. Then something gives, and in the expansion everything happens at once and almost without effort.
