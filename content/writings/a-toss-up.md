In Weimar Germany it was often a toss-up whether a restless young man joined the Communists or the Nazis. The doctrines were sworn enemies, and the recruits were often the same young men.

I want to understand how people get radicalized on either side, because the mechanism has little to do with the side.

## I. Frustration comes first

Mass movements recruit the frustrated before the convinced. The recruit wants out of a self he finds spoiled and a life that feels small. What the movement offers him is somewhere to dissolve: a cause, a uniform, an enemy and a future. The doctrine comes after. Faith in a holy cause stands in for the faith he lost in himself, and hatred binds people faster than hope does.

That explains why the convert is so certain. His certainty is holding up his whole sense of who he is, so he defends it like his life depends on it.

## II. Interchangeable

The radical camps trade members easily. In Weimar Berlin the SA absorbed so many former Communists that people called them beefsteaks: brown outside, red inside. Mussolini edited the Socialist party's newspaper before he founded fascism. The fanatic changes his cause more easily than his temperament.

That is also why the two extremes hate each other most. They compete for the same recruits, the restless and the humiliated, and each knows the other could take them.

## III. Two tenses of the same destination

Both sell a destination, in opposite tenses. Communism promises a future in which contradiction ends and History arrives. Fascism promises the nation reborn after a decline, in the image of a mythic past. One sells tomorrow and the other sells a resurrected yesterday, and certainty about the destination is the tell in both.

Mishima was the aesthetic version of the second. He served an emperor who was a cultural concept more than a man, longed for a Japan that had never quite been what he dreamed, and staged a death to seal it. The soldiers at Ichigaya laughed, and the futility was the point for him.

## IV. The intellectual passenger

The reader is the most dangerous convert, because he needs a more sophisticated substance and finds one. Heidegger's disappointment with modernity was deep and in places accurate. In 1933 he put his university at the movement's service, and his private notebooks, published in 2014, show the antisemitism folded into his thinking. He did more than ride along. He tried to give the movement a philosophy. The disciplined revolutionary on the other side is the same figure, brilliant and certain about where History was going.

Ernst Jünger is the counterexample I keep returning to. He was the idol of the nationalist milieu, the decorated soldier, and his contempt for bourgeois modernity matched theirs. He refused a Nazi seat in the Reichstag in 1927 and again in 1933, forbade the party newspaper from printing his work, and in 1939 published an allegory against the regime. He had the same disgust and he handed it to no leader.

## V. Today's recruits

The type is easy to find now. A young man goes to the gym, reads the Stoics and military history, wants to be a modern Renaissance man, and is disgusted with a world that has stripped life and death of beauty. Much of his diagnosis is right. In his circles the nearest outlet is the radical right. A young woman in a different milieu, with the same frustration, finds the revolutionary left within reach. Neither has a coherent political philosophy. Both want the status quo broken and will join whatever promises to break it.

The duality does the rest. Good and evil, progress and reaction, the right and the wrong side of history: these are mythological poles, the old split between gods and demons now producing political identities. Parties sort people into teams that can be counted and mobilized. Once someone has fused his identity with a team, every position in the bundle comes along, and thinking any single issue through starts to feel like betrayal. Picking a side becomes the point where thinking halts.

## VI. What to do with the frustration

Spain has a name for the people who refused both doors: the third Spain. One of them, a Sevillian journalist, called himself a _pequeño burgués liberal_ and said he had done enough to be shot by either side. He left in 1936 and wrote the best account of the war from a hotel in Paris.

The frustration itself is real, and the honest move is to treat it the way a trader treats the urge to chase a candle. The urge is information about your state and about the crowd's. Acted on, it puts you on someone else's map. Watched, it tells you where everyone like you is about to be.

What stays useful is a stance built from what the movements leave out. Name your own priorities and argue about means. Build leverage that no movement can grant or take away. Refuse to enter the arena on either side's terms.

## VII. Where this pulls against itself

- **My own raw material.** I share the recruit's disgust with modernity and his contempt for the status quo, and _Always Against_ is half in love with rupture. What separates me from him is what I do with it, and I have to keep earning that difference.
- **Horseshoe.** Treating the extremes as one animal is contested, and the doctrines and their records differ in ways that matter. My claim concerns the recruit's psychology. Whether the movements themselves are equivalent is a separate question, and this essay leaves it open.
- **Contempt for the many.** Calling recruits frustrated can turn into the same disdain for ordinary people that the movements exploit. The diagnosis only holds if it stays compatible with wanting those people free.
