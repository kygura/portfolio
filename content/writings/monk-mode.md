The pursuit of happiness is the pursuit of want. Personal happiness is the same drive as desire under a friendlier name. Asking whether it is worth the effort assumes there is a choice in the matter, and there isn't one.

Schopenhauer and the Buddhists are right about the diagnosis. The will produces suffering. Satisfaction is brief, the wanting returns, and craving sits at the root of dukkha. They are making the same point and it holds.

The prescription is where it breaks. Why deny the will? Because suffering less feels better. Renunciation is pursued for relief, and relief is one more thing wanted. Wanting to end wanting is still wanting. Spinoza saw this more clearly: desire is the very essence of man. There is nothing outside it to stand on while you try to switch it off.

So renunciation cannot be decided. It arrives once the desire is spent. Forced early it becomes repression, and what is repressed comes back. The early renunciate also gives up things he never had, and he carries the question of what they would have been for the rest of his practice.

Hesse wrote the whole arc in Siddhartha. The ascetics teach him to fast and wait, and it doesn't take. He has to go through Kamala, the merchant's life, the dice, and finally disgust with himself before sitting by the river means anything. The river works because he arrives there empty.

The Hindu ashramas put it in the calendar. Student, householder, forest dweller, renunciate. Pleasure, wealth and family belong to the householder. The forest comes after the grandchildren. By the tradition's own rules, the young monk skipped two stages.

Enlightenment stays available the whole way through. Most of what happens to you, and most of what happens in the world, is the cycle of suffering at work, and you can choose to abandon it at any moment. Knowing that changes how you live in the middle. You can chase things fully because you see the cycle for what it is, and the game stops owning you even while you play it.

The Gita gives the householder this exact instruction: act completely and let go of the fruits. Karma yoga is renunciation practiced inside the wanting. Zen goes further and says samsara and nirvana were never two places, so walking through the door requires leaving nothing behind.

I can't renounce what life has to offer, and I won't pretend otherwise. That would mean rejecting my nature as a human being and as myself. The choice to abandon the cycle is always open; wanting to make it is what comes last. Monk mode is where I'll end up once I'm honestly done, closer to death, with nothing left to wonder about. Until then, knowing the door is there is enough, and there is a lot to be done.
