There are two ways to hold a belief. You can arrive at it, by working through a problem until a conclusion forces itself on you. Or you can land on it, because someone else's conclusion happened to sound good when you ran into it.

Most people online land. They come across an idea that sounds niche enough and conveniently justifies whatever weak position they already occupy in life, and they attach themselves to it. They become ideological parasites. They live off conclusions someone else paid for and can't produce one of their own.

You can tell the difference fast. Ask someone who arrived at an idea to apply it to a case its originator never covered, and they will, and they'll tell you where it stops working. Ask a parasite and you get the slogan back. They were impressed by the conclusion and never touched the reasoning under it, so nothing in their head connects the idea to anything else. They can't defend it. Most of the time they can't even tell you what it implies.

This is also why they hold it more rigidly than the person who came up with it. A thinker knows which parts are solid and which are guesses. The parasite treats every part as sacred, because the belief is what holds their identity up, and losing a piece of it means losing a piece of themselves. When the thinker revises a position, the tribe either can't follow or turns on him.

The internet makes all of this cheap. No idea is too obscure for a subculture to form around it. Go on YouTube and look at the edits: Hyperborea, lifting-culture fascism, statues and phonk. Or the endless forks of anarchism and communism, egoism, anarcho-primitivism, anarcho-capitalism, anarcho-collectivism, anarcho-whatever. Thirty seconds of music over a Greek bust has no room for an argument, so what gets passed around is a mood. People sign up for the mood and pick the label afterwards.

And the labels keep splitting. Inside an already niche subculture there's a sub-label, and inside that another one, each a way to stand out within a group that exists to stand out. "I'm a communist, but a different kind. An anarcho-bolshevik." Ask what separates it from the parent ideology in practice and there's no answer. The prefix is a jersey. Ask what the position costs them, or what would make them drop it, and you find there are no commitments behind it at all.

Stirner is the clearest case. For years almost nobody online talked about him. Now there are TikToks set to FNAF remix music where teenagers preach the power of saying no and brag about having transcended their spooks. His whole book argues against letting any fixed idea own you, and they turned "egoist" into a group identity with its own sound, its own slang and a flag to defend. They took the one thinker who tells you not to belong to anything and made him the mascot of a tribe. Seeing through morality, nation and duty is the tutorial. They clear it and think the game is finished. The actual game starts once nothing outside you gives orders anymore and you have to decide what you want and live with it, which takes years and happens where no camera can see it.

The people who fall into this mostly think a little above the norm. That half-step is enough to see the holes in the mainstream story, which are real. It isn't enough to build anything in its place, so they grab the first ready-made alternative that feels as clever as they now feel. Before, they deferred to the default. Now they defer to a tribe and believe the idea was theirs, so they defend it harder than any normal person ever defended the default. Getting past that zone means doubting your own conclusions as hard as you doubted the ones you were handed, and most people stop doubting the moment they find others who agree with them.

The cost falls on everyone else. Some of the thinkers these tribes form around are worth agreeing with. But the crowd is so stupid that saying so puts you in it, and getting out of the association takes an explanation nobody wants to hear. So the people who did the thinking keep quiet, and the parasites become the public face of ideas they never understood.
