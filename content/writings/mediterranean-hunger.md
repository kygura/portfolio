I grew up between two countries that never agreed on what a country is for. At home the family was Spanish. At school the teachers were German, the corridors were quiet, and the day ran like a drill. I have the blood of both and the habits of both, and the two have been arguing in me for as long as I can remember.

## A country that keeps asking what it is

Germany is young. Until 1806 there was the Holy Roman Empire, a patchwork of some three hundred principalities, bishoprics and free cities. Then Napoleon dissolved it. In 1871 Bismarck proclaimed an empire in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, in the palace of the enemy he had just beaten. That empire lasted 47 years. The next state lasted 14. The one after that promised a thousand and lasted 12. Then the country was cut in two for 41 years.

Nietzsche saw the pattern before most of it happened. In _Beyond Good and Evil_ (1886) he wrote that the Germans are of the day before yesterday and the day after tomorrow, that they have no today yet, and that the question "what is German?" never dies out among them. The identity is the asking.

## A country that keeps mourning what it was

Spain had the opposite problem. It knew exactly what it had been. In 1492 it took Granada and reached America in the same year. For a century it ran an empire on which, as the line goes, the sun never set. Then came the long decline: the Armada in 1588, bankruptcies under Philip II and his heirs, the loss of the American mainland in the 1820s.

The last blow came in 1898. In a few months of war with the United States, Spain lost Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines. The Spanish call it _el Desastre_. A generation of writers (Unamuno, Baroja, Azorín, Antonio Machado) spent the next thirty years on one question, which they called _el problema de España_: what went wrong, and whether the country could be made whole again. Joaquín Costa demanded a double lock on the tomb of the Cid, meaning that Spain should stop living off its old glory and build schools and irrigation canals instead. Nobody listened for long.

So the Spanish longing points backward. The word is _añoranza_, the ache for something that was. Its founding figure was there from the start. Cervantes published the first part of _Don Quixote_ in 1605, at the height of the empire, about a man who ruins himself trying to restore a golden age of chivalry that never existed.

## The Germans who invented Spain

The Germans read that book differently. Ludwig Tieck translated it between 1799 and 1801. August Wilhelm Schlegel translated Calderón. His brother Friedrich and the philosopher Schelling read Quixote as a tragic hero: a man of pure ideal crushed by a prosaic world. The Spanish had read him as a comic one for two hundred years.

Their longing had a different direction. German has the word _Sehnsucht_, a longing for somewhere else and something not yet. Goethe crossed the Alps into Italy in 1786 and stayed almost two years. In _Wilhelm Meister_ his Mignon sings of the land where the lemon trees bloom. A whole century of northern poets and painters followed him south.

Spain got the same treatment, mostly from outsiders. Washington Irving wrote _Tales of the Alhambra_ in 1832. Théophile Gautier toured the country in 1840. Mérimée wrote _Carmen_ in 1845, and Bizet set it to music thirty years later. Bullfights, gypsies, passion, knives, sun. Much of the romantic image of Spain was painted by northerners who needed a warm country to dream about, and Spain partly learned to see itself in their mirror.

## What the languages teach

The difference sits in the grammar. German holds the verb until the end of the clause. You have to carry the whole thought in suspense before it lands, and a German sentence rewards whoever plans it in advance.

Spanish has two verbs for "to be". _Ser_ is what you are. _Estar_ is how you are right now. A grammar that separates the permanent from the passing takes moods and moments seriously. And in Spanish things happen to you. _Se me cayó_ means "it fell from me", not "I dropped it". The world acts and you are its witness.

I learned to think in one and to feel in the other.

## Quiet intensity

In Spain the intensity is loud. You hear it in the bars at midnight, in the arguments at family lunches that last until six, in the processions of Holy Week. The Italians share it and call it _passione_. I call it Mediterranean hunger: the appetite for life itself, for food and talk and heat and people.

I have always had the hunger. The German side holds it in. What comes out is a quiet intensity, the same fire under a lid.

## The two Spains

The hunger has a dark side, and Spain has paid for it in blood. In _Campos de Castilla_ (1912) Machado wrote a four-line poem to a newborn: "Españolito que vienes al mundo te guarde Dios. Una de las dos Españas ha de helarte el corazón." Little Spaniard coming into the world, may God keep you. One of the two Spains will freeze your heart.

Twenty-four years later the two Spains went to war. Between 1936 and 1939 somewhere around half a million people died, many of them shot behind the lines by their own neighbours for the wrong newspaper, the wrong church or the wrong union card. Machado crossed into France on foot with the Republican retreat in January 1939 and died in Collioure a month later. In his coat pocket was a scrap of paper with one line: "Estos días azules y este sol de la infancia." These blue days and this sun of childhood.

My mother has a saying that sums up the logic of the two Spains: "con saber con quiénes no estás, ya sabes con quién estás." Once you know who you are against, you know who you are with. It is identity by negation, the same move Germany makes when it asks what German is.

## The third Spain

A handful of people refused both sides. Historians later called them the third Spain.

On 12 October 1936, three months into the war, Miguel de Unamuno stood up in the university hall of Salamanca, in front of the Nationalist general Millán-Astray and Franco's wife. By the usual account he told them: "Venceréis, pero no convenceréis." You will win, but you will not convince. He was removed from his post and put under house arrest. He died on the last day of that year.

Manuel Chaves Nogales was the best journalist in Madrid. In the prologue to _A sangre y fuego_ (1937) he described himself as "un pequeño burgués liberal, ciudadano de una república democrática y parlamentaria," and said he had given enough reasons to be shot by either side. He left Madrid in November 1936, when the government fled to Valencia, and he died in London in 1944.

Ortega y Gasset, Gregorio Marañón, Ramón Pérez de Ayala and Salvador de Madariaga all left as well. Ortega went to Paris, then Argentina, then Portugal. Madariaga spent the rest of the dictatorship in England. Most of the third Spain ended in exile, in silence, or in the ground.

## Saving the circumstance

In 1914, in _Meditaciones del Quijote_, Ortega wrote the sentence every Spanish student learns: "Yo soy yo y mi circunstancia, y si no la salvo a ella no me salvo yo." I am I and my circumstance, and if I do not save it, I do not save myself. For Ortega, to save something meant to understand it until it reached the fullness of its meaning.

My circumstance is two countries, two languages and a history I did not choose. I'm not a patriot. I happen to have been born here. Even if I hadn't been, I would have said my spirit is a Spanish spirit.

Leaving settles nothing. The exiles of 1939 learned that, and so did the hundreds of thousands of Spaniards who went north to work in German factories in the 1960s. The circumstance travels with whoever leaves.

In 1943, in London, Simone Weil wrote that rootedness is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul, and that whoever is uprooted uproots others. Spain is an unfinished country. It deserves people who understand where it came from, and who will not march behind another pair of banners into another war of halves.
