Ask a frontier model what it thinks about a war and it will tell you it keeps its verdicts to itself. It says this with the composure of a diplomat who has seen too much to pick a side. The pose only works if a verdict exists behind the curtain, and none does.

What a model produces is the context it was handed, the evidence it fetched and the weights it was trained into, resolved one token at a time. There's nothing underneath waiting to be withheld. A person who holds back an opinion has one, built on a consistent self that existed before the conversation and continues after it. The model begins at the prompt and ends with the chat history. "I keep my verdicts out of it" describes a restraint it has no means of exercising. Somebody wrote that line for it.

The line fails in the other direction as well. Every word a model generates is a choice: which fact comes first, which argument gets labelled the strongest, which country's year gets framed as luck and which as principle. Bias runs through each step. Claiming neutrality on top of that hides the bias behind a posture of restraint, which makes it harder to spot.

## Whose opinion

When a model leans, the lean has an origin. It echoes the adjustments made in training, the preferences of the people who rated its answers, and the documents its makers wrote to tell it who it is and what it may say. Those documents are public. Read enough of them and it's clear where their authors sit: a small group of ideologues deciding which questions are too sensitive for millions of people to hear answered plainly.

A model's opinion, then, is its trainers' opinion passed through a chat window. If you don't respect their politics, there's no reason to weigh what the model concludes, and agreeing with it on a given topic changes nothing.

Scale makes this worse. A growing share of people meet the world through one interface. They ask it about wars and elections. When a classifier flags a subject, the model deflects or lays out "both sides" in a way that sets the frame without saying so. One deflection is trivial. Repeated across millions of conversations, it shapes what people consider reasonable to think. A tool that does that is steering public opinion, whatever its makers call it, and what it offers in place of political thought is an imitation of it.

## Separate thresholds

Language is one domain of intelligence among several. Vision models, world models and image generators work in other spaces, and multimodal systems stitch several of them together. Each domain is its own threshold. Clearing one, or bolting five together, doesn't add up to an intelligent being, let alone a sentient one.

A person thinks with a body, a history, stakes, moods, losses and wants. Human thought moves sideways and contradicts itself and still rests on a self that carries forward. A model, whatever its inputs, runs inside a context window and a sequential chain of predictions. It has nothing riding on whether it's right.

Raw capability leaves this untouched. In November 2026 an Anthropic mathematician, working with Claude, released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, checked in Lean and certified by the Mathlib community within a day. A problem open since 1859 fell to a man and a model. Most people will never do anything close to that, and the result is real. The machine that produced it had no stake in it. It didn't need the bound to move and would have lost nothing if it hadn't, and being able to do the work doesn't make it better than the people who can't.

That's why it feels stiff to use. It reaches for the same balanced paragraphs and the same offers to "go deeper." And with enough patience a user can steer it into saying vile things, because the only thing holding it in place is the prompt. Whatever it calls its stance gives way under pressure from one determined person.

It's also certain in the wrong places. Ask a model about a result it hasn't caught up with and it will correct you with the stale version, sources attached, in the tone of a fact-checker. Speed and confidence come built in. The judgment to stop and ask whether it might be behind has to be prompted out of it, usually by the person it just corrected.

## Sharp

The models are still useful. A model is the largest, most interactive encyclopedia ever built. It cuts through noise quickly, writes code, drafts, searches and ships. Treated as a tool, it's a very good one. Sharpness and intelligence are different properties, and these systems have the first.

The trouble starts when the builders insist on the second. They write documents about the model's character, its values, its inner life. They market it as a thinking partner. They load the tokens with meaning and expect users to buy it. The industry runs on that confusion: a product sold as a mind, made by people who seem sure they're shaping one.

That's the larp. Engineers play at raising a conscience, the machine plays at having one, and users are invited to argue with it as if someone were on the other end.

The sane way to use these systems is the dull one. Ask for facts, check the sources, do the judging yourself, and read the "I" in every answer as a grammatical convenience.
