## I. Equality comes last

Most people who invoke equality have it backwards. It has to be earned, and it only shows up where leverage is spread evenly and people actually feel trust, empathy and some understanding of each other. Those conditions are rare and they don't hold for long. Equality is the last thing a system like that produces, on the few occasions it produces it at all, and treating it as the starting point gets the order wrong.

There is no equality before the law where justice can be bought and the best defense lawyers go to the highest bidder. There is no equality among people who are different and unequally placed from birth. The era, the country, the neighborhood, the family with its money and connections: all of it decides a great deal before you've made a single choice. Then there's what no redistribution touches. Your body, your temperament, the appetite you woke up with. Nobody picks those, and every later choice gets made with them.

So when I say I don't believe in equality, I mean two things:

1. It isn't real. It doesn't describe the society we live in.
2. It isn't worth aiming for. It's too grand and too vague to apply, and too many fixed facts stand in its way.

The idea that we're all equal has done more harm than good, and most people know better anyway. They know it when they choose a school, a surgeon, a lawyer or a neighborhood, and they keep saying the sentence in public because it keeps the peace. Favoring the meek is the Christian ideal. The world favors whoever has leverage.

Thucydides wrote it down 2,400 years ago, through the Athenian envoys at Melos in 416 BC: justice only comes up between equals in power, and otherwise the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. The Melians answered with the gods, with fairness, with the hope that Sparta would come. Athens took the island as the envoys said it would, killed the men, sold the women and children, and sent 500 colonists to live there.

## II. Know your place, then move

Your position is temporary and negotiable. But you have to negotiate from an honest account with yourself: who you are, where you stand, what you actually hold.

The myth of equality tells people they hold cards they don't, then sends them to the table. Anyone who plays seriously reads the table first. Who needs this more, who can walk away, who can wait, who's bluffing and who can afford to be called. Someone who believes he's owed an equal hand reads none of that. He takes the loss as an injustice and comes back next week to lose the same way.

Knowing your place doesn't mean accepting it. The Melians warned Athens that its own fall would bring the worst vengeance, and the envoys shrugged. Twelve years later Athens surrendered to Sparta with its fleet gone and its walls being pulled down. Leverage moves, and the only mobility I trust is following it to whoever kept reading the table after everyone else settled on a story about it.

## III. Justice can't be written down

Who decides what justice is? Depends who you ask, and which age you were thrown into. The people who administer it mostly follow a rulebook with no room for nuance. Some do better, but that isn't the norm and nobody expects it.

This is the old problem of turning justice into law. Justice needs judgment and room to bend. A universal handbook removes both by design. Aristotle saw it in Book V of the _Ethics_: law has to speak in general terms, some things can't be said correctly in general terms, so the law will always fail some particular case. The fault, he says, is in the subject matter, and neither the law nor the lawmaker can fix it. His remedy was equity, a correction where the law's generality fails, and his picture for it is the lead ruler the masons of Lesbos used, which bends to fit the stone.

Take that into a courtroom. A rule that bends needs someone to bend it, and bending costs time, argument and a lawyer good enough to make the judge look at your particular stone. Equity now exists mostly for people who can pay to argue for it.

Nietzsche went back further, to where the word came from. In the second essay of the _Genealogy_ he places the origin of justice among people of roughly equal power who found it cheaper to come to terms, and who forced the terms on those below them. It's the Melian rule told as history. He adds that anything that exists gets taken over in time by stronger forces and bent to new uses, so whatever a word means today tells you who holds it today. Justice has served a tribe, a priesthood, a king, a party and a bureaucracy, and each of them called it eternal.

## IV. When the stories come off

Ask what would have to be true for equality or justice to bind anyone. There would have to be a standard somewhere that holds whether or not anyone enforces it: a God keeping accounts, a natural law, a dignity written into the nature of things, a History headed somewhere. Mackie's point stands. Nothing like that is part of the world. Each of those guarantors is a claim about what can't be checked, and the one commitment I keep is not lying about what can be known.

Take the guarantor away and look at what's left in the room. People, what they want, and what each of them can do to the others. That remainder is leverage. I don't admire it and it doesn't need a theory; it's simply what's still standing once nothing stands behind the words. The void at the bottom of the universe and the Melian rule at the top of politics are one fact seen from two distances. There's no why out there, so there's nowhere to lodge an appeal, so the strong do what they can.

This gets called cynicism or nihilism. I don't care about the label. I'd love to live somewhere basic decency, empathy and understanding were guaranteed. People just aren't like that. Decency exists, and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't, but it exists where people built it and keep paying for it, among those with enough trust and enough rough parity to afford it. Nothing outside backs it.

Over long stretches of time it's hard to say whether things are spiralling up or down. My bet is a slow, steady spiral upward, and I call it a bet on purpose. Once someone is certain where things are headed they've stopped thinking and started doing theology, and the record of people certain about History's destination is written in mass graves. A bet can lose. This one has to be won again every generation, by people doing the work. Nobody is going to win at this point by appealing to anyone's sense of morality or justice. Most people run like automata on their beliefs, their jobs and their routines.

## V. The void

Humans give everything special meaning, of course they do. We're among the most sophisticated things nature has made, and we never stop producing symbols, ideologies and reasons. None of it captures the real texture of the world. Most of what thought produces is noise, every frequency blasting at once. Why would one sequence matter more than the rest?

The universe just exists. There's no why and no reason behind it. You find that in Zen and in Taoist writing, and they share a root with nihilism: the void, absence, the creative nothing.

Stirner opens and closes _The Ego and Its Own_ on a line borrowed from Goethe's "Vanitas!": _I have founded my affair on nothing._ He means it as a boast. God, Humanity, the Nation and Truth all demand your service, and all of them, he says, serve only themselves. Clear them out and what's left is "the creative nothing, the nothing out of which I myself as creator create everything." The Daodejing puts the same nothing to work. Thirty spokes meet at the hub, and the cart is useful because of the empty space there. The hollow is what makes the pot useful. Chapter 5 takes away whatever comfort is left: heaven and earth treat the people as straw dogs.

Nishitani came at it hardest from the other side. He said his life's problem was overcoming nihilism through nihilism. In _Religion and Nothingness_ he treats nihility as a field you have to cross quickly on the way to emptiness, where things return to what he calls their home-ground. I like Zen: the rudeness, the speed, Linji telling his students to kill the Buddha if they meet him on the road. I don't hold it. I don't cross the field, because I haven't seen anything on the far side that isn't another name. A home-ground is still a ground, and I don't accept one. I take what's useful from these traditions and leave them where I found them, which is what Linji asked for anyway.

Most frameworks are a waste, faith-based ones especially. A few at least try to help. Stoicism and existentialism respect the people reading them. They don't promise the universe cares. They give you a tool for building on the void instead of covering it up, and if that's a cope, it's a useful one. I'd rather have a cope I can name than a truth I have to kneel to. Own the want, even if you didn't choose it, and play the game you didn't design with your eyes open.

## VI. Convergence

We're all pointing at the same object from different angles.

Stirner, Laozi, Nietzsche and the Zen masters are cross-sections of one thing, cut at different angles, which is why they come out in different vocabularies. The points of contact are specific. All four go after the fixed idea. Stirner calls it a spook and says the whole world is haunted. The first chapter of the Daodejing says the name that can be named isn't the eternal name. Nietzsche traces every sacred word back to the interest that took hold of it. Linji goes after the most sacred name in his own house. None of them sends you somewhere else for the answer, to a heaven or to the end of History. And all four end up at a single body doing something right now, whether that's enjoyment, non-interference, self-overcoming or eating when hungry.

They split on what you do once you're there. Stirner consumes. Everything is his to use up, himself included, and Nishitani's complaint about him holds on its own terms: Stirner's self-enjoyment leaves out the other person. Laozi steps back from force and gets further by yielding, the highest good being like water, and then Chapter 3 says the sage governs by emptying people's minds and filling their bellies, so the book reads the table as well as anyone. Nietzsche climbs. He needs rank, a higher type, a future worth what it costs, and from Stirner's chair the Übermensch looks like one more spook. Zen dissolves whoever is doing the climbing, then sweeps the floor.

Those disagreements are real and I don't want them smoothed over. Leibniz has the right picture: each monad is a living mirror of the universe, and the same city looks entirely different from each side. Naming breaks the Tao into planes. The traditions are projections, shadows of one object on different walls, and they only disagree about its shape if you forget something is casting them. A shadow also takes its shape from the wall. Here the wall is one body, one era, one temperament: Stirner among the Berlin Hegelians, Linji in a Tang monastery, Nietzsche walking alone at altitude with a ruined stomach.

## VII. Nobody owns the object

Someone will read this and hand me Huxley. _The Perennial Philosophy_ sees the same convergence and does what institutions always do with a discovery: writes down the object's name and description and opens a school. The one truth under all religions becomes another religion. I refuse that step for the same reason I refuse the others. All I've claimed is that these cuts line up. To say in positive terms what they line up on, I'd need a position outside every cut, and there isn't one. Nobody stands outside the wall. I'm working with the same tools I'd have to replace to get a better view.

So nobody owns the object, the Zen masters, the Kyoto School and me included. What the convergence gives me is a test. When a framework says it alone sees the thing, its authority is what's being sold, and I can stop reading. When people with no reason to agree, separated by centuries and languages, each cut down to the same missing why, I take that as evidence about the world, the way I'd take independent confirmation of anything else. Then I use what they found and get out from under them, as each of them would have told me to.

What's left is where I started. No ground and no guarantee, and leverage decides what happens in the room. Inside that you still get to have a shape: a mind that can be located, willing to be wrong in a specific direction.
