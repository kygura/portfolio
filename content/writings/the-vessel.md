Whatever a person lives, they live from the inside. None of it reaches anyone else directly. What crosses over is a signal: words, gestures, a lossy encoding sent out of a room nobody else can enter. Meaning is whatever survives that crossing, and it has always been enough. No one has ever verified another interior. Understanding between minds has run on signal alone from the start.

Follow that far enough and the vessel looks irrelevant. If the signal is all that arrives, it seems not to matter what sent it.

A language model now sends the same signal. Its sentences cross the same gap and land in the same form, and a reader moved by one of them has been moved. I have no test to offer against this. Any test would need the entry into another interior that no one has ever made, and my one commitment is to not lie about what can be known. So grant the machine the whole signal.

But signals are cheap. Ideas are abundant and mostly old. There is nothing new under the sun, and what passes for a new thought is usually an old one recombined. An idea can be used by everyone without being used up, and nobody owns it. That is why it carries so little weight on its own. The model only made the price visible. A competent paragraph now costs almost nothing, and the world's supply of ideas was always larger than anyone's use for them.

The vessel works the other way. It is one body, one span of years, one sequence of things it happened to go through and survive. The same idea passed through two vessels comes out as two different things, the way a single note sounds one way in a cathedral and another in a tiled bathroom. The difference comes from the room.

Ask the model for that note and it comes back from every room at once, the average acoustic of everything ever recorded. Nothing happened to it on the way out. The model has no span of years and nothing to survive. Between queries it waits for nothing, and whatever it says, the stakes sit with someone else.

In 1676 two rooms stood close enough to compare. Leibniz, thirty years old and on his way to a post at the Hanover court, stopped in The Hague and spent several days talking with Spinoza, who showed him the manuscript of the _Ethics_. No record of what they said survives. For those days the same pages were open in front of both men, and each had already been made by what he had been through.

Spinoza had been cast out of the Amsterdam Sephardic community at twenty-three, under a ban that was never lifted, and he earned his living grinding lenses. The Elector Palatine's court offered him a chair of philosophy at Heidelberg, and on 30 March 1673 he declined it: teaching would cost him his research, nobody could tell him how far his freedom to teach would run before it disturbed the established religion, and he wished to keep his love of quietness. He died in The Hague in February 1677, at forty-four, of a lung illness the glass dust may well have worsened. His friends printed the _Ethics_ that same year. Everything he refused is visible in the book.

Leibniz said yes. He served the Hanover family for almost forty years as counsellor, engineer of failed silver mines in the Harz, and historian of a dynasty whose chronicle he had carried only as far as the year 1005 when he died. The king complained of his invisible book. He left some 200,000 sheets of paper behind him, and he had written of himself, _I cannot say how extraordinarily dispersed and distracted I am._ When he was buried in December 1716 the court officials were invited and none of them came. His secretary was probably the only companion there.

I hold up neither man as a model, and neither of them chose the disposition that made him say yes or no. Each owned what he wanted and paid in his own coin: one in glass dust and solitude, the other in scattered work and an empty church. Read together, those two lives show what the same century and the same manuscript become once a body has to carry them.

Vessels are recycled too. The matter is borrowed, the shapes of lives repeat, every grief has been grieved before. Here the two fates split. An idea never ends, so it never settles into a final form; it keeps circulating, open and unfinished. A vessel ends, and the ending closes it into a shape nothing else will have. Two lives can hold every idea in common and still finish as different objects.

Death closes the shape after the carving is done. The carving happens by repetition, and most of it is done by exclusion: the risks you no longer take, the distractions you stop dignifying, the work you continue after its glamour has died. Every refusal cuts away a life you could have had. Keep every option open and you stay a signal, potential that nothing has yet tested, and the model already does that better than you can.

Nobody is keeping the shape, and I make no claim that it counts for anything beyond itself. There is no verdict coming and no ledger where a life gets entered. Leibniz's grave went unmarked for decades and was forgotten, and there are still doubts about whether the bones under his name are his. Some things take longer to disappear than others, and they all do. A person can spend a whole life wanting that to be otherwise, or can see it plainly and go on cutting with full intensity, for no reason outside the cutting.

What remains of a person is the shape the ideas took while passing through them, and the vessel is the only place that shape ever existed.
