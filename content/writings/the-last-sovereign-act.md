The state exists, and it will keep re-emerging however often it's torn down. Nobody signed the contract that binds you to it, yet it collects on that contract every day. What follows reads the terms as they actually operate: what power is, how it speaks, what it owes, what the individual holds against it, and how to live with clarity inside a game you never chose to enter.

---

# Part One: The Nature of Power

Before asking what the state owes anyone, it helps to see what it is: a recurring product of human scale, held together by force, run by people the system selects for indifference.

## I. The State Acts as if Inevitable

The state behaves like gravity. It needs no moral justification to exist. Complex societies generate hierarchies of power, and those hierarchies coalesce into states regardless of intent. Abolish one and something fills the vacuum: warlords, cartels, corporate capture. Strip a state of its claim to legitimacy and it's still a state.

History keeps running the experiment. Revolutions that set out to dissolve authority end by installing a new one, often harsher, because the people who seize the vacuum are the ones willing to use force first. Somalia after 1991 got clan militias and protection rackets in place of a government. The Soviet project promised the withering away of the state and produced one of the largest bureaucracies ever built. Wherever enough people live close enough together, someone ends up collecting tribute and settling disputes, and whatever they call themselves, they function as a state.

The anarchist vision works at the scale of a commune, a crew, a friendship. It fails at civilizational scale because it can't hold its shape. Any position worth holding has to start from the state as a fixed condition of the terrain.

## II. The Social Contract Is Coercive by Nature

No one consents to the social contract. It's imposed at birth, renewed by force, non-negotiable in practice. Living inside a society is a condition you're born into. Calling that coercive is just accurate.

Contract theory papers over this with fictions: tacit consent, the vote, the option to emigrate. A newborn signs nothing. A vote lets you choose between managers of the arrangement, never whether the arrangement applies to you. Emigration moves you from one jurisdiction to another, each with its own unsigned terms, and most people lack the means to leave at all.

The binding happens anyway, and for a reason. The drive to fold individuals into a tribe is written into the species' genetic makeup. Groups that held together outlasted groups that didn't, and we descend from the ones that held. The contract is older than any philosophy written to justify it.

## III. Power Is Amoral by Operation

At the top, morality is a tool. Those who hold real leverage think in outcomes, incentives and positions: what works, what's defensible, what can be gotten away with. Good and evil rarely enter the calculation.

Power selects for this. Whoever hesitates on principle gets outcompeted by whoever doesn't. Ruthlessness at that altitude is the cost of staying there. The filter runs at every rung, in corporations, parties, agencies and armies, so by the time anyone reaches a position of real consequence, the scruples that would have slowed them have been bred out or set aside.

For them, it's just business. For the masses, it's life or death. The same decision that moves a line on a balance sheet upstairs decides who eats, who works, who gets treated and who gets sent to war downstairs. Distance does the rest: the person who signs the order never meets the people it lands on, and abstraction makes cruelty easy to administer.

## IV. Institutional Power Tends Toward Capture

Left unchecked, states drift toward serving those closest to the machinery at the expense of the people they govern. The drift needs no conspiracy. Concentrated interests watch closely and lobby hard; diffuse publics are busy living. A regulation worth millions to one industry costs each citizen a few euros a year, so the industry shows up to every hearing and the citizens never do.

Over time the regulator hires from the regulated and retires into it. Agencies come to see the world through the eyes of the firms they oversee. Laws get drafted by the people they're meant to constrain. None of this requires anyone to break a rule, which is what makes it so durable.

Vigilance against capture has to be permanent, because the pull toward it never lets up.

# Part Two: The Two Ledgers

Every society runs two moral ledgers. One is written down: taught in schools, preached from pulpits, broadcast at scale. The other never gets written down. It's the operating code of the people closest to the machinery, and it reads very differently.

## V. Morality Is the Shadow Cast Downward

What elites disown in themselves gets projected onto the masses as vice, and its opposite gets sold as virtue. Ambition becomes greed when you have it. Self-interest becomes selfishness. Your force is violence; theirs is order.

Jung's shadow at civilizational scale: the ruling class teaches everyone else to be ashamed of the exact traits it depends on. Culture is where the projection lives. Humility, patience, deference, sacrifice for the collective. Useful traits in a population you need to govern. Fatal ones in anyone trying to rise.

Nietzsche read the same split from the opposite direction, as slave morality: the weak recasting their weakness as goodness. Either way the virtues on offer to the many are calibrated to keep them where they are, and those who absorb them most completely are the easiest to manage. A worker who believes asking for more is ugly will never need to be refused.

## VI. Rules for Thee, Not for Me

The double standard is the design. Laws, norms and moral codes exist to make the many predictable. The few write them, interpret them, and buy their way out of them.

Lockdowns for the public, dinners for the officials. Austerity for households, bailouts for banks. Insider trading prosecuted downward, legislated upward.

The pattern holds wherever enforcement is discretionary. Tax codes run to thousands of pages, and the complexity itself is a subsidy: only those who can pay specialists find the doors built into it. A minor offence can ruin a defendant who can't afford a lawyer, while a corporation settles a fraud for a fraction of what it earned and admits nothing. Equality before the law exists in the text and thins out with every rung of wealth.

## VII. Esoteric and Exoteric Ethics

The split that runs through religion runs through politics too. Mystery traditions kept one teaching for initiates and another for the congregation, and power does the same. The inner circle works from the real map: leverage, incentives, narrative control. The outer mass gets the symbol (fairness, duty, the common good) and is told the symbol is the thing.

Most people live their whole lives inside the symbol and call it justice. The public code is there to hold them together and keep them compliant. Whether it's true never comes into it.

The insiders rarely experience this as hypocrisy. Many believe the symbol in public and act on the map in private without ever noticing the seam, and that lack of friction is part of why the arrangement lasts. Strauss argued that philosophers have long written on two levels, one for the few and one for the many. Rulers have always lived that way.

# Part Three: The Terms of Legitimacy

If the state is inevitable and the contract coercive, the useful question is what a coerced party is owed. The answer sets the only standard a state can be judged by.

## VIII. Coercion Implies Obligation

The state extracts compliance through law, taxation, conscription, and the monopoly on violence. That extraction incurs a debt, paid in concrete services that materially empower the people it governs: public health, education, security, infrastructure. This is the minimum owed for a bargain no one agreed to.

The logic is the same as in any forced transaction. Take something from someone without their consent and you owe them, at the very least, a return that leaves them better off than they'd have been without you. A state that taxes heavily and delivers crumbling hospitals, failing schools and unsafe streets is running a protection racket with a flag. The debt stays on the books when it's denominated in services, and it isn't forgiven because the creditor never signed.

## IX. The State's Only Legitimate Function Is Empowerment

The state is valid only to the extent it holds up its end of the bargain: empowering individuals to live with more capability, dignity, and agency than they'd have alone. Beyond that mandate it's overreach, and short of it, extraction.

This rules out most of what states claim as purpose. Forging national identity, enforcing a moral vision, steering citizens toward some collective destiny: none of it is owed, and all of it competes with the one thing that is. A state that builds roads and runs clean courts has earned its taxes. A state that tells you what to believe, whom to love, or what to die for has wandered off the mandate and started spending legitimacy it never had.

## X. The Individual Is the Unit of Value

Political legitimacy flows upward from the individual. Systems and institutions derive their value from how well they expand individual capacity. Any arrangement that compresses agency without proportional return is illegitimate, full stop.

Collectives are real in the sense that they act and have effects, but they experience nothing. A nation doesn't suffer, a class doesn't hope, an economy doesn't feel secure. Only people do. Every appeal to the good of the whole has to cash out eventually in what happens to particular lives, and when the accounting is done honestly, many sacrifices made "for the people" turn out to have benefited a small number of them.

## XI. Liberty Is Measured in Practice

Rights written into law but unreachable in practice are theater. A right to education means nothing without functional public schools. A right to health is hollow without accessible healthcare. A right to speech carries no weight when economic precarity silences people as effectively as censorship, and when even the freest societies still police how free that speech actually gets to be. What matters is operative liberty: the concrete capacity to act.

Constitutions are full of promises that never reach the street. The Soviet constitution of 1936 guaranteed freedom of speech, press and assembly, and took effect as the purges began. Plenty of democratic constitutions promise decent housing to people who now spend half their income on rent. One criterion evaluates any political arrangement: does it expand or compress the individual's operative freedom? Outcomes are the only evidence.

# Part Four: The Sources of Leverage

The individual is where power gets exercised or surrendered, every day. Against an inevitable state and a captured class, an individual's position depends on what can't easily be taken from them: means, knowledge, and the ability to leave.

## XII. Economic Sovereignty Is the Foundation of All Other Freedoms

Political freedom without economic independence is a managed illusion. Someone who can't sustain themselves can't meaningfully dissent, relocate, refuse, or build. Dependence on an employer, a state, or an institution is soft capture: no chains required, just precarity.

Most people sit a few missed paychecks away from losing their footing, and every decision they make is shaped by that margin. They hold their tongue at work, stay in the wrong city, defer the project they actually care about. Savings buy time, and time is what lets someone say no.

For the sovereign individual, financial independence is a prerequisite for having agency at all. That's why compounding, building, trading and creating (asymmetric leverage in whatever form) are the conditions for liberty. The goal is a position where no single employer, client or institution can switch off your livelihood, which makes wealth a means of defence long before it's a means of comfort.

## XIII. Information Asymmetry Is Power

Understand a system others don't, and you have leverage over it. You earned it by putting attention and effort where most people stay passive. Someone fluent in market microstructure, institutional incentives, regulatory capture, or narrative mechanics can move through terrain others are simply subject to.

The people at the top already know this, which is why so much effort goes into keeping systems opaque. Financial products are made complicated so their costs stay hidden. Contracts run long so nobody reads them. News arrives framed before it arrives informed. Each layer of opacity is a margin someone collects from those who can't see through it.

Literacy, whether technical, financial, political, or historical, compounds. Every system you learn makes the next one easier, because the same incentives repeat under different names. Nobody can tax it, confiscate it, or redistribute it away from you, and it crosses every border with you.

## XIV. Exit Is a Fundamental Right

The capacity to leave a job, a country, a system, a relationship, or an ideology is one of the most undervalued forms of leverage there is. Whoever can't exit is captive. Institutions know this and exploit it: healthcare tied to employment, pensions contingent on loyalty, citizenship that functions as fiscal captivity.

Hirschman framed the choice as exit or voice, and showed that voice only gets heard when exit is credible. An employer listens to the worker who could walk. A government courts the capital that could leave. Those with no alternatives get politely ignored.

Exit works even when it's never used. A second passport, a portable skill, savings in more than one currency, a network outside your current employer: each changes how you're treated simply by existing. Cultivating exit options is a moral and strategic imperative. Exit is also a valid form of political speech, and often the only kind that registers.

# Part Five: The Practice of Sovereignty

Seeing clearly is the first half. The second is a way of living that follows from it: building outside captured systems, refusing the guilt they issue, and keeping the whole thing maintained.

## XV. Parallel Construction Over Reform

When institutions fail their mandate, the sovereign response is to build. Route around captured institutions instead of asking them to fix themselves. Petitioning a captured system for reform means asking it to act against the interests that captured it, and it rarely does.

Operating outside means building parallel infrastructure: alternative income, networks, platforms, knowledge. Every system that consolidates power leaves edges and gaps, and the sovereign individual works them out of clarity. The internet, decentralized finance, remote work, open-source tooling: these are exits. Each began at the margins, ignored by incumbents until it was too useful to shut down.

Parallel systems also change the old ones. Once enough people can route around a bank, a broker or a publisher, the incumbent has to compete for them, and competition achieves what decades of petitions couldn't. Where exit isn't available, the pressure has nowhere to go but up, and revolution is what happens then: collapse, death, rebirth, on repeat. Building alternatives is the slower and less bloody path through the same cycle.

## XVI. Seeing the Ledger Is Not Joining It

The aim in recognizing the elite code is clarity. You stop mistaking incentives for morality, stop expecting rules to bind the people who wrote them, and stop carrying guilt that was manufactured to keep you in line.

The risk on the other side is cynicism: deciding that since the people at the top play without scruples, you should too. That trades one borrowed code for another. Learning how power operates gives you the map. Whether you behave the way its holders behave is still a choice, and it's yours.

What you keep is your own code: examined, earned, chosen, owed to no one who profits from your obedience. Ambition, self-interest and force can come back into it without shame, alongside whatever loyalties and limits you've decided are worth keeping.

## XVII. Sovereignty Requires Continuous Maintenance

Sovereignty is a discipline you keep practicing. Skills atrophy. Capital erodes. Networks decay. Attention gets captured by design. Maintaining your capabilities, independence, and situational awareness is ongoing work, and complacency is exactly how free individuals turn into managed ones.

The capture rarely announces itself. A comfortable salary makes the side project feel unnecessary, then the skills that would have supported it go stale, then leaving stops being an option anyone takes seriously. Feeds are engineered to absorb the hours that learning would have taken. Each step looks reasonable on its own, and the sum is dependence.

The collectivist critique has this backwards. The sovereign individual is the precondition for social cohesion. People who think, sustain, and build for themselves produce more genuine value than people who are merely administered. A civilization of sovereign individuals is harder to capture, manipulate, or collapse than one of dependents. Empowering the individual is the social good's highest expression. The idea that everyone must stay preoccupied with collective affairs beyond their control is impossible, and worse, a sickness that subordinates people to ideals that will eventually break them. We base our affairs on nothing.

# Part Six: Against the Available Arenas

Where this stands relative to the existing positions, and why it declines all of them.

## On the Ideologies That Came Before Me

**Libertarianism.** It shares the suspicion of the state and the faith in the individual, then stakes everything on markets. Markets left alone produce just outcomes in theory. In practice they reward whoever already holds capital and information, and without a floor the people at the bottom never reach the starting line. This position wants the floor, and holds the state accountable for building it.

**Statism.** It treats the state as a vehicle for meaning, identity, or collective salvation. The state is a service arrangement with a monopoly on force, and asking it to supply meaning invites it to define what your life is for. Every regime that took on that role ended up demanding sacrifices in its name.

**Reformism.** It assumes captured institutions can correct themselves through internal pressure: better candidates, stronger oversight, one more commission. Some reforms land. Most get absorbed, diluted, or turned to serve the interests they were meant to check, and the faith that the next one will be different keeps people inside the system that's failing them.

**Anarchism.** It sees the coercion clearly and draws the wrong conclusion. Abolishing the state only clears ground for the next one. This position accepts the state as inevitable and conditionally useful, and puts its energy into making it pay what it owes while building around it where it doesn't.

## On the Ideological Temperament of the Parties

The revulsion toward both left and right is aesthetic first, analytical second. The left's dominant cultural mode produces moralization, victimhood signaling, weaponized sensitivity. The right produces nationalism and religious tribalism as identity substitutes. Both demand coalition membership as the price of admission. Both punish independent thought that breaks from the party line.

What's missing is tribal allegiance itself. The hostility is to conformist thinking at scale, whatever the policy. The contemporary left happens to be the loudest expression of managed consensus right now, which makes it more grating day to day. The right's version is more localized but the same animal.

Each side's rituals do the same job: they sort people into teams that can be counted, mobilized and marketed to. Once someone has fused their identity with a party, every position in the bundle comes along, and thinking any single issue through starts to feel like betrayal.

Centrism splits the difference between two poles and calls it balance. This position refuses to enter the arena on either side's terms at all.

## On Political Positioning

This sits outside politics in the conventional sense. I don't operate within the left-right spectrum, and honestly, neither do the parties claiming to represent it. Participation in that arena requires faith in it, and I have none.

The left's diagnosis of extractive systems is largely correct. Wealth concentration is a designed feature. The mythology of meritocracy is a key instrument of labor capture. These observations hold up. Those closest to power have no incentive to share their edge: proximity to leverage, ego, short-sightedness, greed. The banality of evil, basically.

That comes from the nature of people and power. Systems fulfill a function. Bad actors don't care much about stated purposes or claimed ideologies. They're here to get their bag. Power concentrates, and the more you have, the easier it is to get more.

The disagreement is with the remedy. State intervention and institutional reform assume the institution is a neutral corrective force. Usually the institution is the problem. Reform tends to get absorbed, redirected, or inverted by the same machinery it's aimed at.

The operative response is exit and parallel infrastructure. Build systems that route around the gatekeepers, cut dependence on captured institutions, expand individual leverage.

Naive free-market libertarianism doesn't fit either. Markets are neither meritocratic nor just. The closest label is post-institutional: having seen the available arenas, declining to play in any of them on their terms. In practice that means treating a vote, when it's cast at all, as a minor tactical act with no faith attached, and spending the real effort elsewhere: on leverage, on exits, on the parallel systems that will matter when the current ones fail.

# Declaration

The state will keep re-emerging. Power will keep concentrating. The people at the top will keep running one code for themselves and handing another to everyone else. Waiting for that to change is how a life gets spent.

So the work starts with you. Learn the systems you live inside until you can read them. Build leverage, and keep building it. Keep your exits open. Route around the institutions that fail you. Hold your own code, and drop the guilt you were issued to keep you in line.

None of this needs permission, a movement, or a majority. It starts with one person who stops waiting, and it spreads through example more than argument, as others notice that someone near them got out of a trap they'd assumed was simply the world.

This is for whoever has already felt the gap between what they were promised and what they got. Acting on it requires clarity, and the discipline to keep it once the world starts pushing back.

Sovereignty is taken, held, and maintained. Nobody hands it out.
